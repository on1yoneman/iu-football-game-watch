Game Preview · Ohio State

October 13, 2026 · Week 7 scouting report
NO. 4 OHIO STATE (5–1) AT NO. 5 INDIANA (6–0) · OCT. 17 · NOON ET · FOX

How Indiana can beat Ohio State again, and why the rematch may look nothing like December

Ohio State brings an extraordinary passing game and a defense that has quietly become one of the country's hardest to throw against. Indiana brings a 22-game winning streak, two elite receivers and the defensive blueprint that unsettled Julian Sayin last December. The game may turn on a less glamorous question: which offensive line can consistently create four yards?

Ohio State: 5–1, 3–0 Big TenIndiana: 6–0, 3–0 Big TenBloomington · First top-five matchup at Memorial Stadium

Ohio State's road here: one narrow loss, five emphatic wins

The Buckeyes opened with a 56–3 win over Ball State, lost 24–23 at Texas, then beat Kent State 59–3, Illinois 42–19, No. 14 Iowa 31–14 and Maryland 45–16. The Texas result remains the obvious test case: Ohio State can be slowed by a talented defense, but its other opponents have rarely kept the game competitive into the fourth quarter. The Iowa win is particularly relevant because it came against a disciplined Big Ten defense on the road; Maryland showed how quickly the Buckeyes can separate when they seize early-down control.

Through six games, #10 Julian Sayin has 1,772 passing yards and 17 touchdowns in Game on Paper's FBS-versus-FBS dataset. #4 Jeremiah Smith has 916 receiving yards and 11 touchdowns; #25 Bo Jackson has 466 rushing yards. The offense is dangerous because it can move the ball efficiently before producing the highlight play. Smith is the obvious centerpiece, but reducing Ohio State to a one-player scouting report would be a serious mistake. Game on Paper's team profile rates Ohio State fifth nationally in opponent-adjusted EPA/play differential, a measure of performance that goes beyond scoring margin.

Why the running game deserves more respect than its reputation

A revealing October 13 statistical analysis at Land-Grant Holy Land makes a persuasive distinction between explosive rushing and useful rushing. Ohio State averages about 4.9 yards per carry. Roughly one-third of runs gain two yards or fewer, but a majority fall in the productive middle range. Bo Jackson and Ja'Kobi Jackson have combined for more than 800 rushing yards, and Sayin has increasingly contributed with his legs: 17 non-sack carries for 75 yards over the Illinois, Iowa and Maryland games after just four such attempts in the first three contests.

That is not a contradiction of the matchup model, which shows a 57.3% Ohio State passing success rate and 45.0% rushing success rate. Both can be true. The run game may be less efficient than the passing game while still being good enough to protect favorable down-and-distance. The distinction matters for Indiana: it cannot simply invite the Buckeyes to run and assume it has solved the problem. Four-yard runs on first down are a win for an offense with Smith outside.

My read: Indiana's first priority should be preventing easy interior movement with its base defensive front. If the Hoosiers can force second-and-eight without adding a safety to the box, defensive coordinator Bryant Haines gains the freedom to disguise coverage behind his rush. If Ohio State consistently gains four or five yards, that freedom disappears and Smith gets cleaner one-on-one opportunities.

The most useful film is from last December

In his film study of Indiana's 13–10 Big Ten championship win, Eleven Warriors analyst Kyle Jones identified more than a red-zone play-calling failure. Indiana anticipated Ohio State's protection rules, disguised coverages that affected Sayin's decisions, and exposed execution problems in the run game. Jones also noted that Ohio State's second-half adjustments were substantially better. This is crucial: Indiana's 2025 plan is a starting point, not a magic formula that can simply be repeated.

Jones' more recent film work on Ohio State's line, including his September and October 2026 studies, has tracked blocking cohesion and execution issues. The question is whether Indiana's veteran front can create the same uncertainty without revealing the coverage too early. Pre-snap movement, late rotation and selective simulated pressures offer one path. Bringing six rushers repeatedly would be a much riskier choice against Sayin's accuracy and Smith's ability to separate.

There is a second coaching layer. Arthur Smith now coordinates Ohio State's offense, and Cignetti said Monday that the system has new subtleties while remaining highly efficient. Smith's willingness to use motion and multiple-tight-end looks can make a defense declare run fits before the snap. The chess match is whether Haines can change the picture after that declaration. A fake pressure look that becomes a four-man rush, for example, may do more to disrupt Sayin's timing than an obvious blitz.

The matchup that may determine Indiana's offense: Hoover against Matt Patricia's coverage

Ohio State's defense allows a passing success rate of only 31.7%, best nationally in the cited matchup model, while allowing a 41.4% rushing success rate. Those numbers argue against a plan built entirely around downfield shots. Matt Patricia has used varied fronts and coverage disguises to prevent quarterbacks from identifying the easy answer before the snap. Kyle Jones' earlier study of Patricia's structure provides useful scheme context, though Indiana must account for 2026 personnel changes.

Josh Hoover's advantage is his pair of receivers. Charlie Becker has 30 catches, 529 yards and eight touchdowns; Nick Marsh has 29 catches, 528 yards and six scores through six games, according to the Sports Illustrated comparison. Marsh has produced substantially more yards after the catch, while Becker's contested-catch reliability can beat sound coverage. The receivers demand different defensive answers. If Patricia tilts help toward Becker because of last year's championship performance, Marsh can attack isolated coverage; if Ohio State doubles both, Indiana needs the backs and tight ends to win underneath.

But Nebraska offered a warning. Indiana gained only 65 rushing yards and converted one of ten third downs. Cignetti acknowledged those problems Monday while arguing that limited first-half possessions also reduced opportunities to establish the run. The distinction matters: Indiana should not abandon its running identity, but it needs greater variety in its opening calls. A first-down run for no gain followed by a predictable second-down pass is exactly the sequence Ohio State's coverage wants.

My read: The most promising Indiana script is to test inside and perimeter runs early, then use play-action and quick throws to Marsh, Becker and tight end Trevor Gibbs from similar formations. Gibbs played 39 of 46 offensive snaps at Nebraska with Andrew Barker sidelined. Even if the tight end is not the primary target, his release can force linebackers to hesitate and open throwing windows behind them. Indiana must also protect the edges long enough for those route combinations to develop.

Two lines of scrimmage, two different vulnerabilities

On the October 12 Bill and Doug Show, experienced Ohio State reporters Doug Lesmerises, Bill Landis, Marcus Hartman and Patrick Murphy singled out Ohio State's offensive line against Indiana's defensive front and Indiana's receivers against Ohio State's corners as the defining matchups. Those are stronger diagnostic questions than simply comparing overall rankings.

Indiana's Stephen Daley recorded two sacks at Nebraska and has become a reliable source of disruption. Tobi Osunsanmi has recorded a tackle for loss in six consecutive games and a sack in five of them, according to Indiana's official game notes. Pressure from four rushers would let Haines protect the back end against Smith; needing extra rushers would make the coverage math harder. On the other side, Indiana's offensive tackles must handle Ohio State's athletic front without constant tight-end help, or Hoover will lose receiving options precisely when he needs them.

Indiana's defense also has a discipline problem to correct. Nebraska repeatedly gained space early, and Cignetti attributed much of the first-half damage to missed assignments and failures to disengage from blocks. Against Ohio State's motion and receiver talent, a single busted leverage assignment could be a touchdown. Indiana's second-half corrections in Lincoln were encouraging, but they do not erase the warning.

The health question is not a footnote

Dan Hope reported at Eleven Warriors that Ohio State played Maryland without six starters: receivers Brandon Inniss and Devin McCuin, linemen Austin Siereveld and Phillip Daniels, linebacker Christian Alliegro and nickel Jay Timmons. Ryan Day expects most, but not necessarily all, to return. That is not a final availability determination. The identity of the returning offensive linemen in particular could change Indiana's pass-rush advantage.

Indiana's latest completed-game availability report listed Andrew Barker, Khobie Martin, LeBron Bond and Carson Williams out against Nebraska. A.J. Harris and Daniel Ndukwe were cleared from that report; Harris then played 25 defensive snaps. Barker's absence changed tight-end usage, while Martin's continued absence affects backfield depth. None of those Nebraska designations should be presented as a final Ohio State-week injury report. The Big Ten's new game-week availability filings remain the authoritative update when published.

Third down, fourth down and the hidden possession battle

Ohio State converts about half of its third downs, according to the cited matchup data. Indiana's 1-for-10 showing at Nebraska is an obvious concern, but third-down rate is partly a first- and second-down statistic. A team facing third-and-three has a much larger playbook than one facing third-and-nine. Indiana's first-down efficiency therefore matters on both sides of the ball: it must keep Hoover out of obvious passing situations while forcing Sayin into them.

Game management deserves equal attention. Crimson Quarry questioned Cignetti's conservative early fourth-down choices at Nebraska. A top-five opponent changes the cost of giving away possessions. That does not mean going for every fourth-and-short; it means field position, expected points, offensive rhythm and the state of Indiana's defense must all inform the decision. One extra meaningful possession may be decisive in a game where explosive plays are difficult to prevent completely.

Three plausible versions of Saturday

Indiana's preferred game: The Hoosiers generate modest but steady rushing gains, use motion to expose coverage rules, and get Marsh or Becker isolated for a handful of decisive completions. Daley and Osunsanmi force Sayin off his first read without sacrificing secondary help. The game reaches the fourth quarter within one score, where Indiana's experience in close games becomes relevant.

Ohio State's preferred game: Bo Jackson and the backs create second-and-manageable, Sayin takes the available short throws, and Smith eventually wins a one-on-one matchup for an explosive score. Patricia's defense puts Hoover behind schedule, and Indiana cannot sustain drives without another spectacular receiver play. Ohio State's offensive efficiency gradually turns a competitive first half into a two-score lead.

The volatile version: Indiana creates pressure and turnovers, but Ohio State hits multiple explosives. Both teams alternate three-and-outs with quick touchdowns. Special teams, red-zone execution and fourth-down choices outweigh total yardage. Given the receiver talent on both sidelines, this is not an unlikely path.

The assessment

Ohio State deserves a slight edge on the underlying efficiency profile, particularly because its passing offense is elite and its pass defense can take away the easiest answers. Indiana has a credible counter: the exact style of disguised coverage and four-man disruption that troubled Sayin in December, plus a receiving tandem capable of beating good coverage without a perfect offensive day. But the Hoosiers cannot ask Marsh to rescue an offense that repeatedly loses first down. The decisive contest is likely to happen before the spectacular play: at the line of scrimmage, on first down, when the defense decides whether it can stop the run with six or seven defenders and the offense decides whether it can keep the entire playbook available.
