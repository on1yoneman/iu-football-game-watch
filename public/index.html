Game Preview · Ohio State

Updated October 16, 2026 · film-room synthesis and independent projection
NO. 4 OHIO STATE (5–1) AT NO. 5 INDIANA (6–0) · OCT. 17 · NOON ET · FOX

Where Saturday will be won: Indiana’s disruptive front against Ohio State’s improving defense

The Buckeyes want to protect Julian Sayin, find Jeremiah Smith and make Indiana’s offense drive the field without explosive plays. The Hoosiers want to make Ohio State’s offensive line handle pressure without blitzes, restore their running game and force Matt Patricia to defend every inch of the field. This scouting report combines film analysis from writers who cover both programs, their coaching staffs’ comments and an independently calculated score projection.

What the film analysts agree on, and where they differ

Cameron Teague Robinson’s film study for The Athletic identifies Indiana’s defensive front as the matchup’s biggest structural advantage. Land-Grant Holy Land’s Ohio State-focused discussion reaches a similar conclusion, while emphasizing Charlie Becker and Nick Marsh as threats to a Buckeye secondary that must tackle in space. Kyle Jones’s Eleven Warriors film studies supply a complementary view of Ohio State’s evolving line play and defensive tendencies. On the Indiana side, Coach Griff’s Hoosier Tailgate film reviews and Crimson Quarry’s Nebraska breakdown point to Indiana’s inconsistent offensive run fits and defensive tackling in its two narrow conference wins. These are different angles on one central question: can Indiana control the line of scrimmage consistently enough to avoid asking Josh Hoover to beat an elite secondary on obvious passing downs?

When Ohio State has the ball: protect Sayin without losing the run

Robinson’s TruMedia numbers are unusually revealing: Indiana ranks 132nd in blitz rate but sixth in pressure rate. Stephen Daley has 16 pressures and five sacks in five appearances; Tobi Osunsanmi has 15 pressures and five sacks in only 94 pass-rush snaps. Defensive coordinator Bryant Haines manufactures pressure with stunts and twists, sometimes dropping an edge defender into coverage while a different player attacks. Sayin therefore cannot simply diagnose an extra rusher and throw into the vacated zone. His tackles and interior linemen must pass off twisting rushers while he reads a secondary that disguises coverage before and after the snap.

That task is complicated by Ohio State’s offensive-line availability. Eleven Warriors’ account of Ryan Day’s Tuesday press conference says Phillip Daniels was expected to return to full practice, while the staff was still managing line changes. Day acknowledged that Indiana’s defensive front would be among Ohio State’s biggest tests. These injuries are relevant even if the starters return: a line with limited recent work together must communicate against exchanges, simulated pressures and late movement.

Indiana also creates problems before Sayin throws. Ohio State averages only 1.04 rushing yards before contact, 123rd nationally, according to Robinson’s TruMedia research. Indiana allows just 0.09 yards before contact, third nationally, and 2.3 yards per carry overall. Tyrique Tucker occupies blocks so linebackers Isaiah Jones and Rolijah Hardy can attack the correct gaps. If Ohio State is repeatedly facing second-and-eight, Indiana can keep seven defenders in coverage and let its front attack. Arthur Smith’s best response may be to use motion, varied formations, quick throws and tight ends to create manageable downs rather than repeatedly run into the heart of the front.

There is a plausible counter. Robinson notes that Northwestern tight end Luke Dehnicke caught seven passes for 140 yards against Indiana, and Nebraska’s Luke Lindenmeyer caught seven for 45. Indiana’s zone structure has yielded space to tight ends, especially when it is occupied with outside receivers or reacting to the quarterback. Nate Roberts and Mason Williams can exploit that space while Indiana commits coverage resources to Jeremiah Smith. Sayin has improved against pressure, with 20 completions on 36 pressured throws for 395 yards and four touchdowns against one interception, according to Robinson. His improvement matters most if he works through progressions rather than forcing throws to Smith.

Indiana’s other vulnerability is tackling after the catch. Crimson Quarry saw mobile quarterbacks, quick throws and perimeter runs repeatedly challenge the defense. Sayin is not the same runner as Anthony Colandrea, but he can move enough to punish a rush that loses contain. Haines must balance pursuit with lane discipline and force Ohio State to complete several well-executed plays instead of giving up a single scramble or uncovered tight end.

When Indiana has the ball: can the RPO offense force Ohio State to hesitate?

Indiana’s offense is built on a powerful run-pass relationship. Turbo Richard and Lee Beebe Jr. accelerate through narrow openings, and the line’s double teams and climbs to linebackers create the second-level space that turns routine runs into explosives. Indiana’s receivers are unusually committed blockers. Robinson notes Richard leads the Big Ten with ten runs of more than 20 yards. But Nebraska loaded the box, filled gaps and prevented Indiana’s linemen from reaching linebackers. The Hoosiers were held below 100 rushing yards and allowed five tackles for loss. Curt Cignetti acknowledged on Monday that missed assignments and poor third-down execution contributed to the offensive problems.

Ohio State’s front has improved substantially. Robinson reports that after Illinois rushed for 199 yards, the Buckeyes held Iowa and Maryland to 121 combined. Eddrick Houston, Qua Russaw and Will Smith Jr. have become more disruptive, and the linebackers are playing with better discipline. That is the danger for Indiana: if Ohio State can win the first two downs without bringing an extra defender into the box, Matt Patricia can keep his secondary in sound coverage and attack Hoover when the quarterback must hold the ball.

Indiana’s response should be to make those linebackers wrong even when they line up correctly. Hoover is completing 78% of throws inside the numbers, for 862 yards and 11 touchdowns, according to Robinson’s TruMedia breakdown. Quick slants, hitches and RPO glance routes punish linebackers who crash against the run. Becker is a precise downfield route runner, while Marsh is a violent runner after the catch. Marsh averages 8.7 yards after catch, leading qualifying Big Ten receivers, and produced both late touchdowns at Nebraska on catch-and-run opportunities. Those plays depend on leverage, timing and perimeter blocking as much as they depend on raw speed.

Ohio State, however, is not Nebraska. Robinson reports the Buckeyes allow just 4.8 yards per pass attempt and have surrendered only one pass of more than 30 yards. Safety Jaylen McClain has played extensive snaps without a missed tackle, according to Pro Football Focus. A shallow completion is not automatically an explosive gain against this defense. Indiana will have to take some calculated downfield chances, where Hoover has completed 11 of 23 throws beyond 20 yards, to keep the secondary from sitting on the RPO game. Patricia’s likely aim is to make Hoover move beyond his first read and recreate the turnover-producing situations he faced at TCU, even though he has not thrown an interception this season.

Friday film and availability update · October 16

The injury news sharpens Indiana's defensive advantage. Ohio State has ruled out Austin Siereveld and Ja'Kobi Jackson. Phillip Daniels is returning at right tackle after missing four games, with Gabe VanSickle moving inside to right guard. Against a Haines front that creates pressure without blitzing, those changes make the Buckeyes' communication on stunts and twists a particularly important early test. Bo Jackson will handle a larger share of the rushing load. Indiana, meanwhile, has Andrew Barker QUESTIONABLE and Khobie Martin DOUBTFUL on the Thursday report; LeBron Bond and Carson Williams have been cleared from the availability list. Expect Gibbs and Schott to take the tight-end work, but treat the Thursday report as provisional until the game-day filing. Eleven Warriors injury analysis · Inside the Hall report.

Ohio State's offense is more multiple than its season averages suggest. In his October 15 film review, Kyle Jones highlights more creative RPO concepts, new ways to move Jeremiah Smith and a broader menu of Arthur Smith run schemes. That gives Indiana more to diagnose before the snap. Haines can still generate pressure with four, but the secondary must communicate when Smith motions across the formation and linebackers cannot chase every run-action cue, especially with Ohio State's tight ends already identified as a vulnerability.

The Buckeyes' secondary has a clear assignment. Dan Hope's position-by-position comparison identifies Indiana advantages in several trench matchups despite Ohio State's recruiting profile. Land-Grant Holy Land's Friday preview emphasizes Ohio State's exceptional pass-defense efficiency. The synthesis: Patricia should aim to tackle the quick RPO throws immediately and make Hoover complete difficult intermediate routes. Indiana's answer is to use Becker and Marsh vertically enough to prevent the Buckeyes from crowding the short passing lanes.

Discipline could decide a one-possession game. Indiana has committed 20 penalties in six games versus Ohio State's 40, according to IU Athletics' Friday report. The Hoosiers' edge in pre-snap discipline and ball security is a tangible reason to resist treating the Buckeyes' superior recruiting rankings as dispositive. The independent 27–26 forecast below remains a narrow lean, not a certainty; this week's personnel news makes the trench matchups more consequential without justifying an arbitrary adjustment to the published model.

The adjustments that could decide it

First, Ohio State’s tight ends versus Indiana’s linebackers and safeties. If Roberts repeatedly finds the space vacated by Indiana’s pressure looks, Ohio State can sustain drives without depending on explosive runs. If Indiana covers that area while still pressuring with four, the Buckeyes will face a difficult choice between protecting Sayin and sending more receivers into routes.

Second, Ohio State’s defensive tackle play against Indiana’s interior blocking combinations. If Houston and Smith occupy two blockers without being displaced, Pierce and the other linebackers can flow to the ball. Indiana needs early-down variation and occasional misdirection to prevent those defenders from simply attacking downhill.

Third, Hoover’s willingness to challenge coverage. Patricia wants to rally and tackle after short throws. Indiana must prove that Becker and Marsh can win intermediate and vertical routes, even when the initial RPO read is covered. Watch whether Hoover gets to his second progression without a defender in his face.

Fourth, the game’s first few third downs. Cignetti said Indiana went just 3-for-15 on third down at Nebraska. Ohio State’s own offense cannot afford to live in third-and-long against a defense that generates pressure without blitzing. The offense that consistently creates third-and-three rather than third-and-eight will have a disproportionately large advantage.

Robinson picks Ohio State 24–21. That is a credible opposing view, especially if the Buckeyes limit Indiana’s yards after catch. Our model leans the other way because Indiana’s home advantage and its front-versus-run-blocking matchup are enough to offset Ohio State’s modest opponent-adjusted edge. The more important prediction is the game script: relatively few easy rushing yards, multiple long-yardage passing downs, a handful of critical tight-end targets for Ohio State and a late possession decided by whether Indiana can turn a short completion into an explosive play.
